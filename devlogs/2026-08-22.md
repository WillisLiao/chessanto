## Design

`MoveClassifier.classify` is pure win-probability math with no board access at all - it only sees `PlyEvaluation` (score, mate, best-move UCI) per position.
Sacrifice detection needs the board: whose piece was given up, whether the opponent actually took it, whether the position settled in the mover's favor.
That data lives in `ReportInput`, which is already in scope at all three real call sites of `MoveClassifier.classify` (`ReportBuilder`, `GameReplayViewModel`, `PortableReportAssembler`) - all three already call `ClassificationContext.forGame(input:openingBook:)` first, the same function that already computes book and forced-move exemptions from board replay.

Routed to an Opus advisor given this is new chess-domain detector logic that must be judged correct against real positions, not just plumbing - the same category of decision as the prior session's teaching-depth work, and the one place in this backlog explicitly flagged as higher-risk than filtering/rendering.
The advisor's design put detection inside `ClassificationContext.forGame` rather than a separate post-processing pass, so every call site gets brilliant assignments for free instead of needing three separate integration points that could drift - the exact class of bug `forGame`'s own doc comment already exists to prevent for book/forced moves ("a move labelled Book on the Mac and Best on the phone is the same move being described two different ways").

The detection algorithm itself is deliberately conservative, because a false "brilliant" is a worse product failure than a missed one in a codebase whose entire architecture (typed Facts, `FactAuditor`, fixed-template `ReportText`) exists to prevent unaudited chess claims.
Five required conditions: the engine's own top choice (not near-best - only rank-1 agreement is the "sound" the glossary already promises), still 75%+ winning immediately after the move, a 10-point uniqueness margin over the engine's second-best choice (the honest proxy for "this was a find" that stored single-search analysis can actually support - not aesthetic judgment, which this codebase cannot honestly claim), not a same-square recapture, and a settled material deficit of at least 3 pawns-equivalent once the sacrifice is actually accepted on the board.
The 3-point floor is deliberate: it excludes the exchange sacrifice (2 points) and any pawn sacrifice (1 point) on purpose.
Telling a beginner that a routine exchange sac is genius costs more than staying quiet about a real Greek gift.

## Implementation and a real bug it caught

Delegated to a bounded implementation worker with TDD against the advisor's frozen algorithm and thresholds.
The frozen spec measured the settled material deficit by checking two fixed points in the replayed line (2 plies and 4 plies after the sacrifice) and taking whichever showed the larger deficit - meant to catch both immediate acceptance and acceptance after one intermediate move.

Following the design's own instruction to run the detector across the real fixture corpus and read every fire by hand before accepting it, the worker found exactly one fire in the committed Carlsen game fixture - and its own hand-read of the position said it was not a real sacrifice.
Move 55, White offers a rook; the engine's own predicted line has Black's queen capture it and White's knight immediately recapture the queen, netting White a queen for a rook two moves later.
That is a winning tactic, not a sacrifice.
It fired only because the fixed k=2/k=4 window sampled material at k=4 - the instant after the rook is taken and before the recapture - where there is a transient 6-point deficit that clears the threshold, even though the position fully resettles in White's favor two plies later.

Rather than accept a demonstrated false positive as "working as designed" (which the worker had reasonably done, since fixing it was outside its brief), I read `ThemeDetector.punishment` again - the closest existing precedent, and one that had already solved precisely this problem for a different detector.
Its doc comment states the rule directly: material is measured "over the longest even-length prefix, where both sides have had their say," specifically because an earlier, cruder version of that detector inferred false facts from a PV's length or a mid-sequence snapshot.
The brilliancy detector had quietly reinvented the same mistake `punishment` was already written to avoid.

Fixed by replacing the fixed k=2/k=4 window with the longest even-length prefix of the full replayed line - the position once the whole predicted continuation has played out, not a snapshot mid-exchange.
This is a smaller, simpler check than the one it replaced (one prefix length instead of two, no `max` needed), not just a more correct one.
Re-ran the fixture corpus: zero brilliants fire now, and every golden-fixture test passes with its original, unmodified text (the worker's own golden-fixture edits, made to accommodate the false positive, were reverted).
The `brilliantSurvivesAnIntermediateCheckBeforeAcceptance` test - built specifically to prove the detector doesn't stop looking at k=2 - still passes unchanged, since its fixture's predicted line is exactly 4 plies long and the longest even-length prefix of a 4-ply line is still 4.
